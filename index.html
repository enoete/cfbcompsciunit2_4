MODULE 2 · LESSON 1 · SYSTEMS DEVELOPMENT

Nobody builds a house without a plan. Why would software be different?

A life cycle is just the order you do things in, written down on purpose instead of discovered by accident. This lesson is the whole decision: which order, why that order, and what actually goes wrong when a team skips a step.

🏗️ THE BIG PICTURE

Every model is a remix of the same six moves

Before the named models — Waterfall, Spiral, and the rest — there's one generic skeleton underneath all of them. Every model you'll meet today is just a different opinion about how strictly you follow this order, and how many times you're allowed to go back.

🏠 Building an extension on a house

You don't start mixing concrete the morning you decide you want a bigger kitchen. First someone checks whether it's even possible and affordable (feasibility). Then you sit with an architect and say exactly what you want — an island, a window over the sink, room for two ovens (requirements). The architect draws it up (design). Builders lay block and wire it (implementation). The inspector checks the wiring won't burn the house down (testing). You move your pots back in (deployment) — and five years later you're still fixing the tap that always drips (maintenance).

Software runs through the exact same six beats. Only the tools change.

What each phase is actually answering: Feasibility asks "should we?" Requirements asks "what exactly do they need?" Design asks "how will it be built?" Implementation is "build it." Testing asks "did we build it right?" Maintenance is everything that happens for the years after launch — bug fixes, new features, keeping it alive.

🛰️ Same six phases, this time for real — Starlink, start to finish

A feasibility study came first — not a formality, a genuine multi-year regulatory and financial bet that could easily have failed (the full breakdown is further down this page) (feasibility). Real requirements got written down: a terminal an ordinary household could set up with zero technical help, and latency low enough to feel like real broadband, not the 600ms+ lag of older satellite internet (requirements). From there, SpaceX had to design the actual system — low Earth orbit at roughly 550km instead of the traditional geostationary 36,000km, specifically to cut that latency, a custom phased-array terminal instead of an old-style satellite dish, and laser links added in a later design revision so satellites could relay data to each other without needing a ground station directly underneath (design). Implementation meant building an entire satellite factory from scratch and launching batches of roughly sixty satellites at a time on reused Falcon 9 rockets (implementation). Before committing to full production, two prototype satellites nicknamed Tintin A and Tintin B flew in 2018, and a public "Better Than Nothing Beta" let real households stress-test the service in 2020 (testing). The service didn't launch to the whole planet on day one — it rolled out region by region, then opened to general availability, with separate versions arriving later for RVs, boats and aircraft (deployment). And none of it has stopped: satellites are deliberately built to deorbit and burn up after about five years, so SpaceX is permanently launching replacements just to keep the constellation alive, on top of pushing firmware updates and fixing real problems like terminals overheating in hot climates (maintenance).

In practice it reads as Evolutionary Development: an initial working version launched and sold, then genuinely new hardware generations (v1.0 → v1.5 → v2 Mini, laser inter-satellite links added later) released as real-world use — including Ukraine — surfaced requirements nobody wrote down at the start. 🔍 FEASIBILITY

Can we actually build this — and should we?

A feasibility study asks five separate questions, and a project can fail any one of them even if it passes the other four.

🚐 The food truck you almost bought

You can afford the truck (economic). You know how to cook (technical). You're legally allowed to park it downtown (legal). You and your partner can actually run it day to day without burning out (operational). And you can have it ready before tourist season starts (schedule). Fail any one — say the permit doesn't exist for your street — and the other four don't save the idea.

TypeThe question it asks
TechnicalDo we have the technology, skills and infrastructure to build this?
EconomicWill the benefits outweigh the cost — is it worth the money?
LegalDoes it break any law, licence, contract or regulation?
OperationalWill it actually work inside how the organisation runs day to day?
ScheduleCan it be delivered in the time available?
🛰️ Real-world check: Starlink. SpaceX didn't skip this step just because it's SpaceX — Starlink failed or barely passed feasibility in several places before a single satellite flew.
  • Technical: borderline at best in 2015. Putting broadband-grade antennas on thousands of small satellites, and building a terminal a customer could point at the sky themselves with no technician, hadn't been done at that scale. The one thing that tipped it into "yes": SpaceX already owned a reusable rocket (Falcon 9), which nobody else building a satellite constellation did.
  • Economic: a genuinely enormous bet — tens of billions of dollars before meaningful revenue, on a plan to launch over 10,000 satellites eventually. The economics only work because reusable launches cut the cost per satellite dramatically, and because tens of millions of homes worldwide have no other broadband option and will pay for one.
  • Legal: this is the one that nearly didn't pass. Operating a satellite network needs spectrum and orbital-slot approval from national regulators (the FCC in the US) and coordination through the ITU internationally, country by country, plus formal objections from astronomers over satellite brightness and from rival satellite operators over interference — none of that is solved by good engineering.
  • Operational: could a car-and-rocket company actually mass-manufacture satellites and ground terminals, and support customers, at consumer scale? Early on, the answer was "not quite" — terminal supply couldn't keep up with demand, and new customers sat on waitlists for months.
  • Schedule: not optional. The FCC licence itself sets hard deadlines — roughly half the approved satellites had to be in orbit within six years of approval, the rest within nine, or the licence could be clawed back. That's a feasibility constraint written directly into the regulation, not a project-management nicety.

📋 REQUIREMENTS

Finding out what they actually need (not what they said)

Clients rarely describe a system accurately on the first try — not from dishonesty, just because most people have never had to put "what I need" into precise words before. Elicitation is the set of techniques for closing that gap.

🎂 Ordering a custom cake

"Something nice for a birthday" is not a requirement — it's a feeling. A good baker asks follow-up questions (interview), looks at photos of cakes the client has liked before (document analysis), maybe watches how the family actually eats dessert at past parties (observation), and sometimes just bakes a small sample tier to react to (prototyping). Only after that does "something nice" become "three-tier, lemon, no nuts, serves 40, ready by 3pm Saturday."

🛰️ Real-world check: Starlink's requirements.

Some of the original functional requirements: the terminal ("Dishy") has to find and lock onto satellites with zero setup from the customer — no technician, no manual aiming. As satellites race overhead at roughly 27,000 km/h, the terminal has to hand off from one satellite to the next without the connection dropping. The service has to work from a fixed registered location.

Some of the original non-functional requirements: latency low enough to feel like ordinary broadband — roughly 20–40 milliseconds, against 600+ milliseconds for older geostationary satellite internet — plus a terminal cheap enough, and a signal reliable enough through weather, for ordinary households to actually buy it.

Where elicitation actually happened: market research and pre-order demand in underserved rural areas validated that the need was real before full build-out. Then real use did something elicitation on paper never could — in 2022, when Starlink terminals were deployed in Ukraine, soldiers and civilians needed the service to survive electronic jamming and keep working while moving, a requirement nobody had written down in 2015 because the original customer was a rural household, not a combat zone. That's requirements elicitation continuing after launch, not something that only happens once at the start — exactly why models like Evolutionary Development exist.

Verification vs validation — the exam's favourite trap: Verification asks "are we building the product right?" (does it match the spec we wrote). Validation asks "are we building the right product?" (does it actually solve the user's real problem). You can pass verification and still fail validation — a system can perfectly match a spec that was wrong from the start.

Glossary

SDLC (Software Development Life Cycle) — the ordered set of phases a system passes through, from the idea to the day it stops being used.

Feasibility study — a check, before real work starts, of whether a project is worth doing and possible to do (technical, economic, legal, operational, schedule).

Requirement — a statement of something the finished system must do or be.

Functional requirement — describes something the system does (an action, a feature).

Non-functional requirement — describes a quality of how the system does it (speed, security, reliability, usability).

Elicitation — the active process of drawing requirements out of stakeholders (interviews, questionnaires, observation, document analysis, prototyping).

Verification — checking the product matches its specification ("did we build it right?").

Validation — checking the product actually solves the user's real problem ("did we build the right thing?").

Prototype — a rough, often incomplete, working model built to get early reaction before full development.

Stakeholder — anyone with an interest in the system: users, clients, managers, regulators.

Iteration — one repeated pass through some or all of the life cycle, used by models that build the system in cycles rather than once straight through.
